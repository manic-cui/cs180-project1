Image Channel Alignment

Each scan contains B, G and R exposures from top to bottom. Split the height into three equal parts, discard any leftover rows, and convert the pixels to [0, 1]. Keep B fixed and align G and R to it.

For the three small JPEGs, single-scale alignment searches integer shifts from −15 to 15 along both axes: 961 candidates per channel. Choose the shift with the highest NCC score.

a = A − mean(A), b = B − mean(B)
NCC = Σ(a · b) / √[Σa² · Σb²]

Subtracting the mean and normalizing the magnitude reduces the effect of overall brightness differences. Ignore the outer 12% when scoring. Use a fixed interior area that stays valid for every candidate shift.

Stack the shifted channels in RGB order and crop to their shared valid area. This removes pixels wrapped by np.roll; the original scan borders remain.

Pyramid alignment

Large scans need shifts beyond ±15 pixels. Searching at full size is expensive, so first find the shift on a small image, then refine it at larger sizes.

  1. Blur with [1, 4, 6, 4, 1] / 16 along rows and columns, then take every second pixel.
  2. Repeat until the longest side is at most 320 pixels.
  3. Search ±15 at the smallest level. At each larger level, double the shift and search ±2 around it.
  4. Continue to the original resolution. The blur and pyramid are implemented directly.

All 14 course images use the same settings. Alignment takes 0.08–2.04 s per image, including both channels and pyramid construction. The full run including loading and saving took 31.1 s on an Apple Silicon Mac.

Failure

Emir has a wrong red-channel shift. Its blue robe has very different brightness across channels, so the highest NCC score gives a wrong match. Mean subtraction does not remove this color difference. Moving people and water can also leave small color fringes.
